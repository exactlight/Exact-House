"Sell your house as-is!" is on every bandit sign in Wisconsin. But *as-is* has
a real meaning — and a few misconceptions that cost sellers money.

## What as-is actually means

Selling as-is means the buyer accepts the property **in its current condition**
— you make no repairs, offer no credits for defects, and generally don't
warrant the condition of anything. The furnace limping through its 25th
winter, the roof with two layers, the basement that seeps every April: priced
in, not fixed.

What it does **not** mean:

- **You can hide known problems.** Wisconsin sellers generally must still
  disclose known defects on the real estate condition report. As-is changes
  who pays for problems, not your duty to be honest about them.
- **The buyer can't inspect.** Buyers can still look; they just can't demand
  repairs afterward.
- **You'll automatically get lowballed.** A fair as-is price reflects the real
  cost of the work — not a random discount because you seem motivated.

## As-is with a traditional listing vs. a cash buyer

You can list a house as-is with an agent, and for houses needing only cosmetic
work it can go fine. The catch: most retail buyers use financing, and
**lenders have condition standards**. Bad roof, dead furnace, foundation
issues, knob-and-tube wiring — those can sink the buyer's loan no matter what
your listing says. That's why truly rough houses often sit, then sell to an
investor anyway — after months of carrying costs.

A cash buyer skips that entire loop: no lender, no condition requirements, no
repair negotiations after inspection. The as-is price you're offered is the
as-is price you close at — at least with a buyer worth dealing with.

## The take-what-you-want part

The version of as-is we practice includes contents: take the belongings that
matter to you and leave the rest — furniture, appliances, the garage full of
half-finished projects. For inherited houses and downsizing moves, that's
often worth more than a few thousand dollars of price difference.

## When as-is is the right call

- The repair list is longer than your patience or budget
- You inherited a property you don't want to renovate long-distance
- You need certainty and a firm date more than the last dollar
- The house wouldn't pass a lender's condition standards anyway
