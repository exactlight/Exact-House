Inheriting a house is rarely as simple as receiving a gift. There's often a
mortgage still attached, decades of belongings inside, property taxes accruing,
and — if you live out of town — a building sitting empty two hours away. Here's
a practical overview of what Wisconsin heirs deal with, and the options for
selling.

## First: can you sell it yet?

In most cases, an inherited Wisconsin property passes through **probate** — the
court process that transfers legal ownership from the estate to the heirs.
Until the estate's personal representative has authority to act (or the
property transfers by other means, like a transfer-on-death deed or a trust),
you generally can't close a sale. What you *can* do during probate:

- Get the property secured, insured, and winterized
- Gather documents: the deed, mortgage statement, tax bills
- Get offers and even sign a contract, with closing set after probate wraps up

Every estate is different — a probate attorney can tell you exactly where yours
stands. (We're happy to work around a probate timeline; we do it regularly.)

## The costs that don't pause

While the process plays out, the house keeps costing money: property taxes,
insurance (often at a higher vacant-property rate), utilities, lawn or snow
service, and the mortgage if one exists. For many families, this monthly bleed
is what turns "maybe we'll fix it up" into "let's just sell it."

## What about all the stuff?

The cleanout is often the hardest part emotionally and practically. If you sell
the traditional way, the house usually has to be emptied and cleaned before
listing. If you sell to a cash buyer like us, you can **take the keepsakes and
leave everything else** — furniture, boxes, the workshop full of tools. We
handle the cleanout after closing.

## Your selling options

1. **List with an agent.** Usually nets the highest price *if* the house is in
   sellable condition and the heirs can wait a few months and fund any repairs
   the buyer's inspector demands.
2. **Sell as-is to a local cash buyer.** Fastest and simplest: no repairs, no
   cleanout, no showings, close when probate allows. The offer is below full
   retail — that's the honest trade for speed and zero hassle.
3. **Keep it as a rental.** Works when one heir wants to manage it and the
   others are bought out — but co-owned rentals between siblings have strained
   more than a few family holidays.

## If the heirs disagree

When multiple heirs own a house, a sale is often the cleanest resolution: one
number, split per the estate, everyone moves on. A fast cash sale can help
precisely because there's less to argue about — no debates over which repairs
to fund or which offer to hold out for.
